A Devotion

Not Far from the Kingdom

Mark 12:13–34 · three questions in the temple · WEB

13 They sent some of the Pharisees and the Herodians to him, that they might trap him with words. 14 When they had come, they asked him, “Teacher, we know that you are honest, and don’t defer to anyone; for you aren’t partial to anyone, but truly teach the way of God. Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not? 15 Shall we give, or shall we not give?” But he, knowing their hypocrisy, said to them, “Why do you test me? Bring me a denarius, that I may see it.” 16 They brought it. He said to them, “Whose is this image and inscription?” They said to him, “Caesar’s.” 17 Jesus answered them, “Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s.” They marveled greatly at him. 18 Some Sadducees, who say that there is no resurrection, came to him. They asked him, saying, 19 “Teacher, Moses wrote to us, ‘If a man’s brother dies, and leaves a wife behind him, and leaves no children, that his brother should take his wife, and raise up offspring for his brother.’ 20 There were seven brothers. The first took a wife, and dying left no offspring. 21 The second took her, and died, leaving no children behind him. The third likewise; 22 and the seven took her and left no children. Last of all the woman also died. 23 In the resurrection, when they rise, whose wife will she be of them? For the seven had her as a wife.” 24 Jesus answered them, “Isn’t this because you are mistaken, not knowing the Scriptures, nor the power of God? 25 For when they will rise from the dead, they neither marry, nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven. 26 But about the dead, that they are raised; haven’t you read in the book of Moses, about the Bush, how God spoke to him, saying, ‘I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob’? 27 He is not the God of the dead, but of the living. You are therefore badly mistaken.” 28 One of the scribes came, and heard them questioning together, and knowing that he had answered them well, asked him, “Which commandment is the greatest of all?” 29 Jesus answered, “The greatest is, ‘Hear, Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one: 30 you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your mind, and with all your strength.’ This is the first commandment. 31 The second is like this, ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself.’ There is no other commandment greater than these.” 32 The scribe said to him, “Truly, teacher, you have said well that he is one, and there is none other but he, 33 and to love him with all the heart, and with all the understanding, with all the soul, and with all the strength, and to love his neighbor as himself, is more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices.” 34 When Jesus saw that he answered wisely, he said to him, “You are not far from God’s Kingdom.” No one dared ask him any question after that.

The text

Three questions, in the temple. The first is a trap, and Mark says so: Pharisees and Herodians are sent “to trap him in his talk”. The second comes from Sadducees. Jesus answers the first with a coin they have to fetch, and the second from the passage about the bush, where God names himself the God of three men long dead. He tells the Sadducees twice that they are wrong.

The third question is different. A scribe who has seen that Jesus “answered them well” asks for one commandment; Jesus gives two, from Deuteronomy 6 and Leviticus 19. The scribe repeats them and adds something of his own: this love “is much more than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices”. Mark does not say what the things of God are in the first answer, or whether the scribe, told he is “not far from the kingdom of God”, came the rest of the way.

Tim Keller

“Mercy, Not Sacrifice”, a sermon on Matthew 9:9–17, preached in 1995. Read from a machine-made transcript. It is not a sermon on Mark 12, and Keller does not mention the scribe; setting it beside the scribe's line about sacrifices is this page's doing, not Keller's.

Keller's subject in this sermon is what he calls religion: bringing sacrifices so that God will owe a good life in return. Its sign, he says, is a low anger when life does not go as it ought. Against it he sets the two commandments, briefly: look, he says, at what God actually requires.

if God really created you, then you owe him everything.

He calls them “utterly rational things to ask”, and says nobody does them. To look at the love God requires is to be humbled. Then the turn: the only mercy that saves is Christ's own. “Don't look at your sacrifices. Look at mine.”

In another sermon, “Jesus and Politics”, on Mark 15 (also a machine-made transcript), Keller stops for a minute on the coin: Caesar's image is on the money, “but God's image is on you.” That reading is at least as old as Tertullian of Carthage, who wrote in On Idolatry: “to render to Cæsar indeed money, to God yourself.” In Mark, Jesus does not spell it out.

The tradition · Bernard of Clairvaux

On Loving God, chapter 10, with one line from chapter 1. A treatise, read from the CCEL edition, which names no translator. He quotes the commandment from Luke, not Mark.

Bernard opens the treatise with its measure: “the measure of love due to Him is immeasurable love.” God's first claim on that love, he says, is that he loved first and gave himself. He then describes four degrees of love, the last being to love even oneself only for God's sake. Of the great commandment he is frank. In this life, he thinks, it cannot be fully and perfectly obeyed:

For here the heart must take thought for the body; and the soul must energize the flesh; and the strength must guard itself from impairment.

So he looks to the resurrection. The soul may hope for this love “only when it has been clothed upon with that spiritual and immortal body”, and even then it is not reached by effort; it is God's to give. Bernard has neither episode in view, but here the two things Mark sets side by side meet: the resurrection and the great commandment. His language is a contemplative's: to reach this state, he writes, is “to become godlike”, though the “substance will endure”. He and Keller both say that no one, in this life, keeps the command whole. Keller, in his sermon, turns from there to Christ's sacrifice; Bernard, in this chapter, to longing for the body raised.

Paul

Paul's word for paying what is owed in Romans 13:7 is the Greek verb behind “Give back” (“Render”, in older translations) in Mark 12:17. In the next breath the language of owing becomes the second commandment. He quotes Leviticus here, not Deuteronomy.

1 Let every soul be in subjection to the higher authorities, for there is no authority except from God, and those who exist are ordained by God. 2 Therefore he who resists the authority withstands the ordinance of God; and those who withstand will receive to themselves judgment. 3 For rulers are not a terror to the good work, but to the evil. Do you desire to have no fear of the authority? Do that which is good, and you will have praise from the authority, 4 for he is a servant of God to you for good. But if you do that which is evil, be afraid, for he doesn’t bear the sword in vain; for he is a servant of God, an avenger for wrath to him who does evil. 5 Therefore you need to be in subjection, not only because of the wrath, but also for conscience’ sake. 6 For this reason you also pay taxes, for they are servants of God’s service, continually doing this very thing. 7 Therefore give everyone what you owe: if you owe taxes, pay taxes; if customs, then customs; if respect, then respect; if honor, then honor. 8 Owe no one anything, except to love one another; for he who loves his neighbor has fulfilled the law. 9 For the commandments, “You shall not commit adultery,” “You shall not murder,” “You shall not steal,” “You shall not covet,” and whatever other commandments there are, are all summed up in this saying, namely, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” 10 Love doesn’t harm a neighbor. Love therefore is the fulfillment of the law.

“The things that are God's.” What are they?

The scribe is told he is not far. What lies between not far and in?
